# Calibration: C and sMax

Final values and the measurements that establish them. Exploration that led here is
deliberately not included - only the runs a reader would need to check the numbers.

Both stores were calibrated after the harness fix described under *Harness corrections*
below. Measurements taken before it do not transfer, because the fix changed throughput
and latency on every topology.

## Hardware

C and sMax are properties of the machines as much as of the workload, and the experiment has run on two CloudLab node types. The configs carry the Utah values; everything below this section is the Clemson calibration, kept because it is the full derivation and because the Utah values were obtained by the same method.

## Scaling the server count

The scale configs (`make_scale_configs.py`, EXPERIMENT.md *Scaling the server count*) carry the five-node C, phase rates, sMax and cap at every size. The Java store's replication budget is measured per size instead: every admitted write is replicated to all N - 1 followers whatever its concern, so a budget measured against four followers does not carry over, and at 27 servers the five-node 105,000 put the leader past its replication stream. The method is the five-node one. A write-only probe (strongest arm, budget and cap out of the way, 30 s steps) finds the leader's peak stream to its followers, and the budget is 0.78 x that peak per follower, rounded down to a multiple of 5,000. The candidate then runs the compressed day (every phase at a third of its length) with the weakest arm, which acknowledges every write on append and so admits at the budget. It passes when the stream keeps pace with admitted writes x followers through SaleDay, PreFlash and Flash with batches below the 4,000-entry cap; otherwise it steps down by 5,000. The check is needed because the mixed load caps the stream below the write-only peak: at 27 servers, chameleon at 64,000 fell behind in SaleDay (stream 0.82 to 0.94 of admitted x 26, batches at the cap through PreFlash) and lost 40% of its profit.

| Servers | Followers | Peak stream, entries/s | Per follower | Budget |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2 | 283,100 | 141,500 | 110,000 |
| 5 | 4 | - | 136,600 | 105,000 |
| 9 | 8 | 933,800 | 116,700 | 90,000 |
| 15 | 14 | 1,542,900 | 110,200 | 85,000 |
| 21 | 20 | 2,090,300 | 104,500 | 80,000 |
| 27 | 26 | 2,126,000 | 82,000 | 60,000 |

Every candidate passed its check on the first try: the stream matched admitted writes x followers (ratio 1.00 in every 15 s window from SaleDay through Flash) with mean batches of 310 to 880 entries. Past the knee the probe's stream readings vary by up to 15% from step to step (15 servers: 1.32M to 1.54M), so the probe gives the candidate and the check confirms it. At 27 servers commits kept tracking the offered rate past the stream's peak, since a majority needs only the fastest 13 followers; the stream is the ceiling that keeps every follower current. The 27-server probe predates the commit-index change of 2026-10-02 (the leader now commits the quorum-th largest match index instead of walking down from the highest); repeated after it, the stream held 2.15M entries/s, which gives the same budget. The other sizes were measured on 2026-10-03, 3 with 21 and 9 with 15 servers running side by side on disjoint machines. mongod keeps its five-node 60,000 at every size: a majority write waits on at most seven voting members, and the 27-server check runs (EXPERIMENT.md) matched five servers.

## Method

**C is a workload parameter and is common across topologies; sMax is per-topology.**
C appears in the configs only as the phase schedule's multiplier base; sMax is the only
system setting that differs between the three topology configs outside the geo matrices.

**sMax is an operating point, not a capacity to discover.** The earlier derivation - the
primary's unregulated in-flight at C, measured with sMax pinned out of reach - no longer
measures anything. With the price silenced, in-flight has no equilibrium: it grows until
something external stops it, which on both stores is `ingressHardCapInFlight` (on mongod as `chameleonHardCapInFlight`; the driver pool bounds it first only at the far global sites). Both give the cap's value back, not the machine's.

**Realized profit is the criterion.** Two alternatives were tried and rejected on
evidence:

- *Goodput* (served minus SLA violations) rises monotonically with sMax on the Java
  store's local and global topologies, so it always selects the largest value on the
  grid. The SLA deadlines (500-2,000 ms for writes) are loose against these latencies
  (11-86 ms), so queueing never registers as a violation and goodput sees only the cost
  of rejecting, never the cost of queueing.
- *Utilization* is tautological. u read **0.85 at 14 of 15 points** of the Java store
  sweep, because the controller drives u to uTarget for any reachable sMax. u is useful
  only as a ceiling test: it falls below target when sMax exceeds what the machine can
  fill, which is how global's overlarge values were caught.

Profit prices both sides of the trade-off. On the Java store's local topology, profit per
request falls 10.75 -> 10.27 as sMax rises from 400 to 12,800, while throughput rises
877,548 -> 991,501/s: queueing pushes responses down the SLA rungs, so total profit peaks
in the middle.

**A price that does not regulate disqualifies a value**, whatever its profit. If u never
reaches uTarget, lambda decays to `lambdaMin` and stays there for the whole run including
overload, which is the priceless ablation rather than the chameleon arm.

**Why these three and not the argmaxes.** The profit curves are flat near their optima -
about 3% across a 2-4x range of sMax - while run-to-run variance reaches 5-19%, so the
argmax is not resolvable. The chosen set is therefore monotonic in inter-node delay,
which the physics supports and which costs at most 2.2% profit (regional 7,200 against
1,800's 9.68M). A majority write holds its slot for the round trip to the second-nearest
follower - 0.6 ms on local, 20 ms on regional, 80 ms on global - so slot demand rises
with delay. Measured write latency at the chosen values is 28 / 107 / 191 ms.

Too small a budget does not merely reject writes, it drives the scorer off strong
consistency: at regional sMax 1,800, 56% of writes executed at the weakest level against
100% at majority at 7,200.

Global is 10,000 rather than 20,000 because 20,000 drives u to 0.78 with lambda at its
floor - the machine cannot fill the budget, so the price never rises.

## Java store: C = 1,000,000

Price-silenced probes tracked 95.8% / 98.7% / 96.8% of 1,000,000 on local / regional /
global, and 93.5% / 96.4% / 97.0% at 1,200,000, so capacity is 1.1-1.2M on all three and
**barely varies with delay** - the framed transport multiplexes over 16 shared connections
per (site, server), so latency costs it no capacity.

At 1,000,000 with the calibrated sMax values all three track 93-98% with 100% of served
writes meeting their deadline, and the rung repeats within 0.3-0.9 points against 11.6
points at 1,200,000.

**2.5C = 2,500,000 is the binding constraint on C, and it is close to the edge.** A single
client machine at `injectorThreads: 30` emits 2,499,371/s (local), 2,491,616/s (regional)
and 2,497,747/s (global) against a 2.5M target, skipping 0.08% at worst; at 3,000,000 all
three fall short by 3-10%. At the previous `injectorThreads: 15` the client topped out at
~1.9M and produced only 75.5% of a 2.5M target, so the Flash phase would silently have run
at 1.9C. 45 threads is worse than 30 (2.74M against 2.92M at a 3.0M target).

Knees at 95% tracking: global **135,000**, regional **250,000**, local **>=250,000**.
Global binds, and 120,000 leaves margin where 135,000 does not.

A repeat of the global fine probe on a fresh allocation gave 96.1% at 120,000 (0.9% pool
drops) and 95.3% at 135,000 (2.3% pool drops), against 96.6% / 1.0% and 95.3% / 2.4% the
first time, so the global knee reproduces within half a point.

**Why global binds, and why the Java store's C does not vary this way.** Every mongod
operation holds one pooled connection for its whole latency, against a fixed
`maxPoolSize: 1000` per (client site, server), so capacity falls as latency rises. At the
same offered rate of 150,000 the three differ almost entirely in write latency:

| | write lat | read lat | writes/s/site | primary-pool occupancy | of the 1,000 cap | pool drops |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| local | 13.3 ms | 8.2 | 3,898 | 52 | 5% | 0.0% |
| regional | 77.3 ms | 35.6 | 3,248 | 251 | 25% | 0.0% |
| global | 304.7 ms | 64.9 | 3,173 | 967 | **97%** | 4.3% |

The write rates are identical (same mix), so the 19x spread in pool occupancy is pure
write latency, set by the distance to the second-nearest follower: 0.3 ms, 10 ms, 40 ms
one way. Pool drops begin once occupancy passes roughly 60% of the cap and grow steeply
past 100%.

Local and regional stop for a different reason, which is why their knees nearly coincide
rather than scaling with their very different write latencies: both tip into positive
feedback around 250-280k, where checkout queueing inflates write latency, which raises
occupancy further. Local's write latency rises 13.3 -> 159.4 ms between 150k and 250k
while regional's rises only 77.3 -> 114.2 ms, so despite starting 5.8x lower local arrives
at the same place.

**Two limits worth knowing when reading any mongod result:**

1. Above roughly **270,000/s** all three plateau regardless of topology (peaks 275,822 /
   273,631 / 279,741) and the loss becomes driver pool refusal - 59-60% `ClientDropped` at
   700,000 offered, while mongod's own in-flight *falls* as offered rises (local 542 ->
   136). That is starvation upstream of mongod, so no larger C is measurable on one client
   machine, and absolute throughput comparisons between the two stores are partly a
   comparison of the client against itself.
2. At **2.5C = 300,000** every topology is past its own capacity, which is the intent, but
   on global most of the refusal is pool checkout failure rather than the price declining
   work. `ClientDropped` must be reported as its own channel and never folded into price
   rejection. Pool drops are not value-aware: at global's top regulated rung a class that
   was 32.0% of offered load took 51.8% of the drops while one that was 14.0% took 1.0%,
   the skew following which (site, server) pools saturate.

**The mechanism differs between the two topologies, and the rule is the same.** On global
the primary's per-site connection pool (`maxPoolSize` 1,000) is the true limit: at sMax
2,000 the price lets the primary's in-flight reach 1,700, the Virginia pool saturates behind
it (2,300 checkout drops per second; 15,600 at 4,000; 2 at 1,000), and every majority-level
upgrade the scorer buys then queues in front of the writes. At 1,000 and 1,500 in-flight
stays at 850 to 1,275 and the pool never queues. On regional the pool is idle at every
value (90 to 770 drops per second) and the slot budget itself is what refuses work, so
profit rises with sMax until lambda approaches its floor (2,400: 1.6e-2, drops climbing),
which is global's 4,000 one step earlier. In both cases the measurements point at **the
largest sMax at which the price still regulates and the pool has not begun to queue**:
global 1,500 (lambda 9.5e-2, in-flight 1,275, drops flat at 19k/s, and 805k/s on
HolidayPlateau against 759 to 779k at 1,000), and on regional 1,800 (lambda 1.2e-1 to
2.4e-1, in-flight 1,560). Local stays at 120: its primary pool runs at 5% occupancy, so
neither mechanism applies, and it was not re-measured on the day mix.

**Regional is held at 1,200 rather than 1,800 to keep sMax increasing with delay**, 120 /
1,200 / 1,500. The measurements alone do not support that ordering on mongod: the pool
caps global at 1,500 regardless of distance, and regional would take more. It is kept as a
modelling choice, and its cost is bounded: 1,200 earned 3.49M/s in SaleDay against 1,800's
two-run mean of 3.69M, about 5%, inside the 12% those two runs differed by. 1,200 also
remains the dwell sweep's own choice.

Profit per request falls from 120k to 300k on every topology - local 10.60 -> 8.11,
regional 10.26 -> 6.59, global 7.44 -> 5.31 - so overload does not merely serve fewer
requests, it serves them at lower value.

## mongod: build provenance, overhead and the client transport

The tables behind this section are in MONGODB_BASELINES.md (sections 1 to 3h), all measured on 2026-09-16 and 17 on one Clemson node with every process on that node; the conclusions are what the paper needs.

**Every reported mongod number comes from an optimised build.** The patched tree is built with bazel's `--config=opt` profile, and the profile is part of the build id so a cached binary of the wrong profile is never reused. The first smoke and probe runs were unknowingly made with bazel's default `fastbuild` profile, an unoptimised debug binary with assertions compiled in: standalone on the same node it cost 0.65 ms of CPU per point read and peaked at 58,000 reads/s, against 0.08 to 0.11 ms and 280,000 reads/s for the same tree built opt and 328,000 for the MongoDB 8.2.12 release tarball. Nothing measured with it is reported.

**The patch costs nothing measurable while idle, and 4 to 5% of saturated throughput while working.** A control build of the unpatched pinned commit with the same profile reproduces the patched binary: standalone at 32 threads, control 204,036 point reads/s at 0.104 ms of CPU per operation against ours 203,927 at 0.105, and 196,532 against 198,270 at 85% reads; a five-node replica set at 128 threads, control 218,289 reads/s at 0.125 ms against ours 195,977 at 0.128, and at 512 threads 200,628 at 0.125 against 215,482 at 0.126. An interleaved A/B of three rounds each put ours ahead in four of eight cells and behind in four, every range overlapping, with CPU per operation equal to the third decimal. The release tarball is 12 to 20% faster than the unpatched pinned commit (254,966 standalone reads/s at 0.080 ms; 233,136 in the replica set at 0.107 ms), which is the release branch plus MongoDB's profile-guided optimisation, both absent from our build and unrelated to the patch. Under the harness, the hooks doing their work, the measurement plane every arm pays for, cost 4 to 5% of saturated throughput on that node (about 174,000 served against 182,000 to 184,000 with the hooks off, at 210,000 to 230,000 offered) and nothing below the knee (served equal to offered through 170,000 with the hooks on or off, and identical under the global delay rules through 120,000); hardware counters at saturation put instructions per served request within 2% of the control. All five arms run the same binary with the plane on, so the cost cancels in the comparison.

**On mongod the client transport is part of the capacity, and it is why sMax rises with delay.** The MongoDB driver holds one pooled connection, and mongod one thread, per in-flight request for its whole latency; the framed transport of the Java store multiplexes over a fixed set of connections and its occupancy is server-side queueing. Under the global delay rules on one node, mongod's knee moved from 170,000 requests/s (no delays) to 100,000 to 110,000, with per-request CPU unchanged and 10 to 15 times more connections and threads open at the same rate, while the Java store under the same delays served 195,900 of 200,000 offered. The same model sets sMax: it is the primary's in-flight at capacity, and by Little's law that is the rate times the residence time, which on mongod includes every replication and read-concern wait. On the single Clemson node the residence times at C were 6.8, 66.7 and 71.2 ms for local, regional and global and the unregulated in-flight 120, 1,281 and 1,509, which is where the Clemson values came from; on the Utah machines the dwell ladders gave 180, 1,800 and 2,250. The Java store's ladders are flat within 2% across a factor of four on every topology, so its rising values are a modelling choice, and mongod's are not: on global, one step above the chosen value the primary's per-site pools saturate a step early in Flash and the transport, not the price, sheds.

The pool cap itself was set from a three-point probe on the global topology at 120,000 offered (weakest arm, 20 s at 80,000 then 40 s at 120,000):

| maxPoolSize | served/s at 120,000 | mean latency | connections on the primary | driver incidents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 111,700 | 218 ms | 6,036 | none |
| 2,000 | 88,100 | 525 ms | 12,037 | none |
| 4,000 | 75,300 | 682 ms | 17,755 | 6 nodes marked UNKNOWN, 206,000 checkout timeouts |

The smaller the cap, the more is served at every rate and at lower latency, because a larger cap lets the latency excursion at the knee grow in-flight, connections and threads together until every pool sits at its cap (six sites times the cap per mongod). 1,000 per site and node is the value in every MongoDB config, with the driver allowed to open connections up to the cap at once rather than two at a time (the default, under which a far pool grew by four to six connections per second against a demand of hundreds and produced 250 to 700 checkout failures per second below the knee; with the change, zero).

Two findings about what the price can and cannot see on this transport. First, a refusal costs the client what a success costs, one pooled connection for a full round trip, and costs the server almost nothing, because the scorer refuses before any wait. In the first chameleon day sequence at sMax 450, at 1.8C the pools completed 131,485 round trips per second of which 9,323 were served, the primary's utilization fell from 0.85 to 0.71 as load rose, and the price dropped to its floor while the followers refused 99,567 requests/s: load raised 2.25x, useful throughput down 9x, with the pools busier than ever. That is what set the two transport rules the sweep runs under, a request is refused before dispatch when its pool is full and its queue is longer than its remaining deadline, and the client's own RTT estimator excludes time it spent queued in the transport, together with the sMax rule (the largest value at which the price still regulates and the pool has not begun to queue). Second, the scorer's budget must include that queue: the envelope's `rtt` carries the current checkout wait of the pool the request will use (*Harness corrections* below), without which the scorer over-predicted realized profit by 26% in SaleDay on global. Even with both, mongod global's Flash in the Utah sweep is bounded by the pools and not by the primary (u 0.6 with the price on its floor while 19% of offered requests time out at checkout), which is recorded under *Known gaps*.

## The price controller: windup, and why lambdaMax exists

Admission keeps a level only while `expected > lambda * omegaMs` (RungScorer step 4f), and
the largest rung profit in the SLA table is 150, so any lambda above `150 / omega` already
refuses everything and more of it changes no decision. The controller does not stop there.

Under overload, in-flight is bounded by `ingressHardCapInFlight`, not by the price - a
request occupied a slot from ingress until its rejection reply went out, so at 2.5C the
pipeline stayed full of about-to-be-rejected requests and u was pinned at `cap / sMax`
whatever lambda did. The error term `u - uTarget` therefore stayed positive permanently
and the multiplicative update compounded unopposed. That is integrator windup, made
explosive by the multiplicative form. The slot accounting that fed it was a defect against
DESIGN.md step 5 and was corrected on 2026-10-13 (below, *The occupancy slot opened at
ingress*); the measurements in this section predate the correction and describe the
mechanism as it was.

Measured on local with the 60,000 cap (`u = 18.75`), during a 60 s phase at 2.5C:

| t into Flash | u | in-flight | lambda |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 s | 0.42 | 1,329 | 0.98 |
| 10 s | 18.74 | 59,964 | 1.4e36 |
| 20 s | 18.74 | 59,974 | 3.6e113 |
| 40 s | 18.74 | 59,977 | 2.6e227 |
| 50 s | 18.74 | 59,964 | **Infinity** |

`exp(0.1 * (18.74 - 0.85))` is 5.98 per 100 ms tick, about 10^38 every five seconds.
In-flight never moves: the price rose by 300 orders of magnitude and changed nothing about
the quantity it controls. Infinity is absorbing under a multiplicative update exactly as
zero is, so that leader then refused every request for the remaining 41% of the run - 7,695
of 18,623 leader intervals at infinity, while all four followers stayed at the price floor
and kept serving. Raft never intervened and should not have: the node held leadership,
heartbeat and replicated throughout, and answered every request, with a rejection. The
fault was in the admission layer above Raft, which Raft cannot observe.

Regional and global reached 1e167 and 1e130 without overflowing, because their `cap / sMax`
was 8.33 and 6.00, and recovered only after ~460 s of refusing everything.

`chameleon.lambdaMax: 1.0e6` bounds it. It changes no admission decision - even an
implausibly fast omega of 0.01 ms puts saturation at 15,000, so 1e6 clears it by ~67x - and
it makes the saturation recoverable, since recovery is proportional to `log(lambdaMax)`.
Verified: no infinity in any phase of six runs across two caps and two arms, with lambda
returning to its 1e-4 floor during Collapse.

The clamp masks the windup rather than curing it. Under saturation the error stays positive
whatever the price does, so the integrator will always run to its bound; clamping makes
that harmless. Curing it would mean not integrating while the price is saturated - the
textbook anti-windup condition - which is a change to the mechanism under study and has not
been made.

**Observed in the Utah sweep.** The clamp was reached in 4 of the 18 Java-store runs of the two price arms, all in the chameleon arm and all under the Flash step: global on every seed (the price at 1e6 for 19 to 27 s of Flash, first reached 8 s into the phase and last left 27 to 56 s later) and local seed 7 (53 s of Flash and the first 11 s of Collapse). chameleonPileus never went above 7 on any topology and the mongod price arms never above 15. The leader's per-second counters in `server_drain_0.csv` show what the clamped state is. On local seed 7 about 348,000 requests/s reached the leader during Flash, `ExecutionAdmitted` was 0 for the whole minute, `ScorerRejected` about 340,000/s, and the admission queue sat about 7,000 deep with a 23 ms wait. That wait is what holds the state: a request occupies its slot from ingress admission to its reject reply, so 340,000 rejections/s waiting 23 ms is u = 2.4 with nothing executing, the error term stays positive and the price stays clamped until the offered load falls. Seed 42 of the same cell passed through the same state for 20 s and left it only because its queue wait happened to fall to 3 to 8 ms, which took u under target and let the price decay from about 430 to 4 over the rest of the phase; it still served only 3% of its writes during Flash. The followers are unaffected throughout (each serving about 222,000 reads/s at a 0.2 ms queue wait), which is why the whole-run totals hide it: local chameleon seed 7 finished 1.5% above seed 42 while its Flash profit was 9% lower (35.1M/s against 38.5M/s), and global chameleon's three seeds are within 2.5% of each other. Three cures would each be small: the anti-windup condition above; not counting as slot time the queue wait of a request the scorer then refuses; or a `lambdaMax` derived from the SLA table so that the cheapest level is never priced out and the cap alone sheds. The second was made on 2026-10-13, once the random family showed the same state in every 80% write phase; see the next paragraph. The Raft runs of the two price arms made before it (the day sequence, the scalability and failure families, and the first random configs) are superseded and are being repeated; the fixed arms never read the price and stand, as do the mongod runs, whose patch opens the slot inside the command path after mongod's own queueing and never showed the state.

**The occupancy slot opened at ingress.** `KvIngressServer` opened a request's occupancy slot when the frame passed the hard cap, before parsing and before the admission queue, and closed it at the terminal reply, so a frame the scorer then refused held a slot for its whole queue wait. Step 5 of DESIGN.md opens the slot on admission. Under write-arrival overload the backlog alone fills the budget: in random config 2's 80% write phase (400,000 writes/s offered against a 105,000/s replication budget) the leader's admission queue held about 2,700 frames at an 11 ms wait, against a target occupancy of 0.85 x 3,200 = 2,720, so u sat at 1.35 with 2,452 requests/s executing, the price at 1e6 for 110 of the 120 s, and 932 writes/s served, while the fixed arms, which never read the price, filled the replication budget at 105,000 writes/s. Rejecting could not lower u because u was made of the frames being rejected. The correction (`KvResponseSink.admitted`, called at the four execution-admission points of `KvClientService`) opens the slot at admission, backdated to the socket timestamp so that an admitted request's slot time is unchanged, queue wait included, as the sMax calibration assumes; a refused frame holds nothing. With it the same phase settles at u 0.85, lambda about 2, and 41,000 to 47,000 writes/s, the slot budget rather than the price being what then binds. The cap-ratio table above was measured with the old accounting, in which in-flight included that backlog, and its 1.5x and 18.75x rows describe the old mechanism.

## Harness corrections these numbers depend on

**Delay-class deduplication in `run_all_cloudlab.sh`.** The geo setup previously created
one `htb` class and one `netem` instance per (src, dst) pair. The cost is contention among
the per-class instances, and it hits the fastest topology hardest, because netem's
per-dequeue cost is amortised over resident packets (~5 on local against thousands on
global). Emitting one class per distinct delay fixed it: local's client went from 25
classes to 2, and the failure mode it caused - majority writes expiring against their
deadlines on the *local* topology - disappeared. Any measurement predating this is void;
on local it moved write latency from 602.3 ms to 27.7 ms.

**`NETEM_LIMIT` stays at netem's default of 1,000.** Raising it to 50,000 was measured and
is harmful: the queue stops signalling congestion, so TCP never backs off. Netem drops
fell 0.315% -> 0.045% but tracking at 700k fell 96.0% -> 79.6% and the age of an expired
frame rose from 2.6 s to 22.6 s mean / 89 s p99.

**`ingressHardCapInFlight = 3 * sMax`, and the ratio is the thing that matters.** In-flight
cannot exceed the cap, and `u = in-flight / sMax`, so `cap / sMax` bounds the largest
utilization the price controller can ever observe - which decides whether it can reach
uTarget at all. Measured on local (sMax 3,200) at three ratios, at 1C and 2.5C:

| cap | ratio | 1C: cap rejects | 1C: price rejects | lambda at 1C | lambda at 2.5C | u at 2.5C | profit/s W+P+F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4,800 | 1.5x | 7.72% | **0.00%** | floor | 1.66 | 0.98 | 29,402,978 |
| **9,600** | **3x** | 1.04% | 0.41% | 3.0e-1 | **235** | **0.78** | **31,081,570** |
| 60,000 | 18.75x | 0.00% | 6.09% | 6.6e-1 | **saturated at lambdaMax** | 0.91 | 23,138,475 |

At 1.5x the cap does all the admission and the price never engages. At 18.75x the price
engages but u reaches 18.75, so the controller can never reach target, saturates, and
rejects 72% at the scorer - serving 557,086/s against 1,518,371/s at 3x. Only at 3x does
lambda stay four orders below its ceiling while holding u at target, and return to its
floor when the load drops.

3x also earns more while serving *less*: at 2.5C it serves 1.52M/s against 1.5x's 1.70M/s
and earns 12% more, because profit per request is 9.84 against 7.85 - the price selecting
what to keep rather than the cap shedding blindly.

An absolute 60,000 was used briefly and was a mistake, chosen from the chameleon arm at 1C
alone. **It cripples the arms that have no price**, where the cap is the only admission
control: strongest at 1C ran 92.5 ms with 17.2% violations and in-flight averaging 54,596,
against 22.8 ms and 0.0% at 3x. Comparing a regulated arm against baselines hobbled that
way would not be a fair experiment, and the defect is invisible unless the baseline arms
are inspected.

In-flight remains heavy-tailed - max/mean over 100 ms intervals reached 9.6x on local at
sMax 3,200 - so a 3x cap does clip spikes (1.04% at 1C). That clipping is the price of a
controller that can reach its target, and `lambdaMax` keeps the consequence bounded.

**`replicationLingerMs: 5.0`**, measured on local after the dedup fix: against 1 ms it
raised C from 800,000 to 900,000, cut leader-to-follower messages from 3,048/s to 744/s by
growing the batch from 135 to 580 entries, and *lowered* mean write latency (18.8 ms
against 21.1 ms at 800,000). 10 ms is worse, collapsing tracking to 71% at the top rungs.
Held at 5 ms on all three topologies; the regional and global halves of that comparison
were not measured separately against 1 ms.

**The injector now counts budget it never offered.** A worker that falls a full tick behind
re-anchors its schedule and drops the missed ticks, which is correct - injecting them late
would burst-load a struggling system - but it was previously unmeasured, so a phase the
client could not generate silently became a smaller one. `Skipped=` now appears in the
progress and completion lines, and a shortfall above 0.01% of configured load prints a
warning. This is what established the 2.5M emission ceiling.

**`transport` is required.** It previously defaulted to `framed` when absent. A generated
config that had lost the line ran a full mongod calibration ladder against the Java store,
with every other key valid and the numbers plausible enough to analyse. Those three runs
were discarded.

**Machine clocks are checked before a distributed run starts.** The servers compute a request's age and remaining deadline from the client's wall-clock stamps against their own clock, so the machines have to agree to well under a deadline. On a fresh allocation one server booted 228 ms fast, and chrony was slewing it back at roughly 30 ms per five minutes. That node expired 605,000 of the 627,000 requests/s it saw on arrival, and because an instant rejection looks like the fastest replica it drew most of the reads to itself; the retries reached the other servers with their deadlines already spent. Writes, which go to the leader, were unaffected, so the three local sMax runs that hit it completed with 83% violations and served throughput that scaled with sMax - a plausible-looking result. `run_all_cloudlab.sh` now refuses to start when any machine is more than 2 ms off NTP time, `setup_cloudlab.sh` steps the clock after provisioning, and those three runs were discarded and repeated.

**The Raft log is bounded.** It is in memory and was never compacted, so every admitted write stayed on the heap of every node for the whole run: a `LogEntry` (index, term, key, value, opId) per write, about 200 to 300 bytes. The servers run with the JVM's ergonomic maximum heap, 30 GiB on the 188 GB CloudLab machines, which is about 145 million entries. In the first day-sequence sweep on the shipping configuration every Java-store run reached that point between 1,060 s and 1,770 s, depending on how many writes the arm admitted: the 100 ms occupancy stream then shows stop-the-world pauses of about 1 s on all five nodes in the same minute (identical logs, identical heaps), growing to 2 to 20 s on the leader, against a 5 s election timeout. Leadership was lost for the first time after 143.5M to 145.5M served writes on local and regional and 119M to 123M on global, in every one of the nine runs that lost it; the other six ended below that count. No election happened in any run before the pauses began, so overload alone never destabilised Raft. The 15 Java-store runs are void, the 15 mongod runs (capped oplog) are not. `RaftLog` now compacts committed entries behind a retention window (`server.logRetentionEntries`, 1,000,000) and a follower whose next entry is compacted is restored from the leader's committed KV state over a new InstallSnapshot stream.

**The mongod envelope's `rtt` now includes the pool checkout wait.** On the mongod transport the client grades a request from dispatch, but the driver holds it in a per-(site, server) connection pool before mongod ever sees it, and mongod starts its clock at arrival. The client's RTT estimate deliberately excludes that wait (charging the client's own queueing to every node once made every node look unreachable), so the scorer's rho, the budget already spent outside the server, never contained it. On global, where the primary's pools saturate, the scorer therefore promoted checkout reads to causal-majority believing 60 ms was attainable and realized 152 ms, and predicted 15.2 profit per request in SaleDay against 11.2 realized (26% over; 6% on local, 9% on regional). The transport now adds the current checkout wait of the pool the request is about to use to the envelope's `rtt`, per pool, so a saturated primary raises rho only for requests bound for the primary. Validated with four 135 s dwells at the SaleDay rate and mix on global mongod, chameleon arm, old and new transport each run once on each allocation:

| | old transport | new transport |
|---|---|---|
| prediction error (realized against predicted) | -27%, -27% | -13%, -10% |
| profit per second | 1.75M, 1.92M | 1.89M, 2.14M |
| pool drops per second | 27,800, 27,900 | 22,700, 20,300 |
| expired in transport per second | 8,700, 2,000 | 500, 1,300 |
| far-site exploration reads reaching the primary (Ireland) | 688/s | 58/s |

Prediction error, drops and expiries move consistently; profit averages +10% with a run-to-run spread of about 10% on a dwell this short. The mongod chameleon and chameleonPileus runs of the first sweep predate this change; weakest, strongest and pileus do not consult the envelope's rho and are unaffected.

## Known gaps

- **The price can clamp shut under the Flash step** (the section above): 4 of the 18 price-arm Java runs, for 19 to 64 s each, the leader admitting nothing while the followers keep serving. The cost is bounded by the phase, under 2% of a run's profit, but it is a property of the controller as implemented and has not been changed.
- **mongod global's Flash is bounded by the driver pools, not by the primary.** In the three chameleon runs the primary sits at u 0.58 to 0.61 during Flash with the price on its floor while 19% of offered requests time out at pool checkout (Tokyo and Singapore first), and in chameleonPileus at u 0.31 with 26% timing out. The price never sees that load, so on this topology the Flash result of the price arms measures the driver's per-site pools as much as the server.
- **mongod global's fixed arms lose secondaries for seconds at a time in Flash.** The driver marks a secondary UNKNOWN after a socket timeout from a far site, 5 to 9 s per episode; strongest lost 19 to 48 requests per run and served 15% of Flash, pileus 63 to 65%. The same shape as on Clemson.
- **The sMax ladder points are single runs on both node types.** The day sequence has three seeds per cell; the dwell probes that chose sMax do not.
- **Admission is a threshold on every request class**, so a small move in lambda can flip a whole class (on Clemson, a 26% change moved three write classes and 8.6M requests in one dwell). Across the three Utah seeds the price arms spread by at most 6.0% per cell (global chameleonPileus), the fixed arms by up to 11.5% (local pileus), so the effect is present but small at the shipping sMax.
- **The scaling runs carry the five-node sMax, and on the Raft store the per-node controllers then have two settled states in Flash.** At 15 and 21 servers strongest earns more than chameleon; at 9 and 27 it does not. With sMax 3,200 on every node, the followers at 15 and 21 saturate on linearizable reads waiting for read-index rounds (u 0.97, about 3,080 in flight, 85 to 96% CPU idle), their price rises and the flash checkout read is downgraded from linearizable (70) to causal-majority (52); at 9 and 27 it is the leader that prices high instead. Reproduced on two seeds with a compressed schedule. Re-measuring sMax per size would change the cap (3x sMax) for every arm and so void every scaling run, so the runs stand as measured and the mechanism is reported.
- **mongod's occupancy clock starts after queueing.** On mongod `t_recv` is the CurOp start, so time a request spends in the kernel or waiting for a service thread before that is in neither its service time nor its slot time; under the scaling configs a primary at 100% CPU read u 0.89. The Java server stamps receipt in the network handler. It did not cause any arm difference seen and is unchanged.
- **At 15 servers the single mongod client machine runs at 85 to 93% CPU in every arm, and some runs degrade.** The signature is eventual-local reads on secondaries during SaleDay far above their healthy few milliseconds (5 to 11 ms against about 2) together with pool checkout drops; chameleon showed it in three runs at 15, pileus mildly in one. Refuted as causes: netem's client queue (a larger one was worse), the pool-wait discount in the routing RTT, the number of active paths, the cross-zone share. Every run of every arm is checked for the signature and for driver topology events the same way; a flagged run is rerun once on other machines and otherwise reported with the flag.
- **The leader's replication stream during a clamped Flash is not explained.** Local chameleon seed 7 streamed about 265,000 entries/s (16 MB/s) to its followers for the minute in which it admitted no writes, and seed 42 the same once its writes resumed at a tenth of that rate; no compaction, snapshot or log-mismatch message appears on any node.

Resolved by the Utah sweep: the mongod price-arm runs now postdate the pool-wait fix and the two parameters of 2026-09-25; the Java day sequence ran with the bounded log (88M to 112M admitted writes per run against a 1,000,000-entry retention window) and no run lost leadership; and the 3x cap has been driven to 2.5C on every topology, where the price holds u at target in chameleonPileus everywhere and in chameleon except for the clamp episodes above.
